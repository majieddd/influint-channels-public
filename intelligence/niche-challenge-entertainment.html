Refuted retention · checked across 6 channels
A long-form video should target ≥60% average percentage viewed; hitting that threshold is what causes YouTube to push the video to broader audiences, after which the measured retention decays as the audience widens.
Source: CreatorNow
What would settle it

What is the video's RUNTIME and format, and is the 60% figure a distribution gate or a descriptive correlate of a specific challenge-entertainment format at ~10-15 minutes? A 60% APV on a 12-minute challenge video and on a 52-minute build video are entirely different demands on the viewer. Restated: (a) within one channel at one roughly fixed length and format, a rising APV is a real improvement signal; (b) no APV number is a cross-channel or cross-length gate on distribution.

Evidence and caveats →

Tested — contradicted: 0 of 119 videos reached 60% APV. Per-channel maxima: Channel C 55.0%, Channel A 54.0%, Channel G 47.4%, Channel F 47.3%, Channel D 41.3%, Channel E 38.7%. Channel median APVs are 31-48%. These same channels post median first-28d views of 54k-988k and produce breakouts up to 1.6M, so broad distribution demonstrably happens far below the stated threshold — and the breakouts sit at the LOW end of their channel's APV (Channel C's breakout 24.8% APV vs 48.3% for the rest).

Caveat: The claim is scoped to challenge-entertainment; none of our six channels is in that niche (maker-build, motorcycle-travel, Roblox gaming), and challenge content with 8-15 min runtimes could plausibly clear 60% where a 23-52 min build video cannot. So the threshold may be attainable in its home niche. What is contradicted is its use as a general long-form target and the implied gate on distribution — our channels are pushed broadly without ever approaching it. Our APV is the unweighted mean of a 100-point curve, a close but not exact match to Studio's APV.

Personally, I'm always shooting for 60 percent retention.

strategy

Theory strategy · checked across 2 channels
One long video beats splitting the same footage into several shorter ones, because a single title-and-thumbnail pair concentrates the packaging and part-twos are not reliably watched.
Source: CreatorNow
What would settle it

Does this footage support SIX DISTINCT PROMISES, or one promise cut into six pieces? Khare is right about PARTS — a part-two inherits a weakened package and asks the viewer to have seen part one. The packet's six are not parts: 'packing' and 'campsite ranking' are separate questions with separate titles, separate thumbnails and partly separate audiences, and none of them requires the others. So the rule is not about count at all, it is about whether each upload has an independent, clickable promise that stands alone. The store also supplies the failure mode at the other extreme: Channel I's c112e6452da9 — 30 daily uploads bought a subscriber bump then flatlined by day 30 as reach fell back to the core (~125k average down to 30-40k), which is what happens when the pieces have no independent promise. our strategist's 5e3bd00115b5 (2-4 long-form from a month-long trip) sits between the two and is the operating compromise.

Evidence and caveats →

Tested: 28 videos carry a part marker but they collapse into only 2 same-stem multi-part sets. Moto Camp Nerd's gift guide: part 1 = 5,457, part 2 = 6,645. Motovudu's Q&A series parts 8-18: 3,745 / 3,992 / 3,079 / 3,396 / 3,248 / 3,658 / 4,860 / 5,566 / 5,075 / 3,608 / 4,274 — flat across eleven consecutive parts, with no part-two cliff. Median part2/part1 ratio 1.14 (n=2).

Caveat: Two comparable sets is nothing, and both are on small channels (median views 5,000-14,000) where audience behaviour may differ entirely from the challenge-entertainment context the claim comes from. The claim's real test is total watch time for one long cut vs a split, and we hold no watch-time data. Multi-part series are also usually signalled outside the title, so the regex undercounts.

Rejected strategy · checked across 12 channels
A monthly upload cadence is compatible with tens of millions of views per video when production quality carries the concept - upload frequency is not a prerequisite for algorithmic success.
Source: Derrel Eves
What would settle it

What share of this channel's views arrive through the subscription/home feed versus suggested and search? Joos's mechanism is ROUTINE — a viewer's habit decays, which only costs you views if your distribution depends on that viewer returning. Eves states the other half himself (3d7710fb7636): consistency 'matters far less once traffic comes from search or recommendations.' The second missing variable is which metric is being defended: our strategist's 7c02437e3d5d says returning/core viewers correlate with upload VOLUME, not quality, so a monthly channel structurally cannot build a large core — it buys outlier reach and forfeits the returning baseline. Ask: am I trying to raise the floor (returning share) or the ceiling (outliers)? Cadence buys the floor.

production

Theory production
Video length should be set by story completeness, not by a target duration — Khare's team shipped a 41-minute video because that was the shortest complete version of the story, and Yes Theory extended a planned 20-minute Wim Hof video past 40 minutes for the same reason.
Source: CreatorNow
What would settle it

Is this an EPISODE or a TENTPOLE? Hurwitz's 5cb5e965b040 already draws the line: regular uploads are episodes of an open-ended series, tentpoles are finite films, and the two require different structures. An episodic container has slack — the metric can legitimately set the dial because the format can absorb another segment. A tentpole's length is determined by its payoff, and cutting to hit a number breaks the thing people came for. Our own data supports the audits' direction within episodic catalogues: the longer half of the catalogue won on median first-28d views for six of seven channels (Channel C 321,662 vs 291,465; Channel D 65,205 vs 48,143; Channel A 251,961 vs 218,645; Channel F 1,012,676 vs 963,861; Channel B 287,063 vs 207,873; Channel G 485,039 vs 367,785). The single exception is Channel E — the channel with by far the longest median duration at 52 minutes — where longer LOST (691,316 vs 863,878). That is the shape of a permission-to-lengthen rule with a channel-specific ceiling, not a universal one, and it is why the rule must be re-read each quarter rather than applied once.

Warning production
'Shoot everything and find it in the edit' is a failure mode of unscripted and spectacle content; going in with a thesis makes the questions you ask and the shots you take more useful, even though the plan must stay malleable.
Source: CreatorNow
What would settle it

Does the payoff exist independently of the shoot, and can this production afford coverage? Khare's format is a designed challenge with an engineered payoff — the outcome is the plan, so shooting without a thesis means missing the shots the payoff needs. Yes Theory's subject is a PERSON or a PLACE whose behaviour is the content, and no thesis can predict it. The economics discriminate too: Brag's 3f346d2d855c says Yes Theory shoots 30-40 mini-scenes and keeps ~18 — a coverage ratio that makes discovery affordable. A single-run challenge with one take cannot buy that option. And Brag himself supplies the guard rail (c07117b89994): the Iceman documentary had to be fully recut because an editing team forced real events into a planned arc — so the failure both sources agree on is a plan that survives contact with contradicting footage.
