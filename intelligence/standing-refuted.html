Evidence and caveats →

Tested — contradicted: Brief: superlative delta -0.06 (maker-builds, n=21), -0.01 (motorcycle, n=48), -0.05 (roblox, n=51) — negative in all three niches. What would settle it

WHERE ON THE EXPOSURE CURVE is this format for this audience? Gannett already holds the reconciling pattern (115f4acc5069): liking follows an inverted-U against exposure count, rising with repetition to a peak and falling after, so a format has a finite number of uses. Evidence and caveats →

Tested — contradicted: There is no mid-video sag to explain. Median hazard falls monotonically from the opening and bottoms out in the middle: 1-5% band 7.0-13.1%, 6-15% 1.1-2.4%, 16-30% 0.4-1.1%, 31-45% 0.2-1.2%, 46-60% 0.0-1.1%, 61-75% -0.1 to 0.8%, 76-90% -0.3 to 0.8%, then 91-100% back up to 0.5-2.9%. Caveat: Two honest qualifiers. (1) The absolute audience does keep declining through the middle; what is flat is the per-present-viewer exit rate. (2) These are cohort medians; individual videos certainly do sag mid-way, and our own analysis flags 99 drop moments in the 30-60% band. The causal half (that a static middle is what produces sag) is untestable regardless — we do not label segment content.

Refuted retention · checked across 6 channels
The single highest-leverage edit target on a retention curve is the point where 50% of viewers have left; pushing that point later in the video causes YouTube to recommend the video more, because it raises average view duration.
Source: Derrel Eves
What would settle it

Is the comparison WITHIN one video (same audience composition, before-vs-after an edit) or ACROSS videos in a catalogue (audience width varying)? Eves is asserting a counterfactual on a fixed audience; Joos is describing a cross-sectional correlation in which audience breadth is the confounder and dominates. Restated conditionally: (a) holding reach constant, later 50%-departure raises AVD and is worth editing for; (b) across a channel's catalogue, retention-at-50% is a proxy for audience narrowness and predicts FEWER views, so it must never be used as a cross-video ranking of quality.

Evidence and caveats →

Tested — contradicted: rho(retention@50%, first-28d views) was negative in 5 of 6 channels: Channel C -0.45, Channel D -0.51, Channel E -0.85, Channel A -0.47, Channel F -0.89; Channel G +0.07. Pooled within-channel -0.50. APV vs views: negative in 6/6 (-0.46 to -0.83), pooled -0.54. The link survives the duration control: within duration tertiles pooled rho = -0.49; residual-method rho negative in 6/6 (-0.39 to -0.85), pooled -0.51. The proposed mediator does not carry either: AVD-in-seconds vs views is ~null, pooled -0.11 (per channel -0.49 to +0.15). Breakout videos (>=2x channel median views) had LOWER median APV than the rest in 6/6 channels (e.g. Channel C 24.8% vs 48.3%, Channel A 21.9% vs 42.9%).

Caveat: This is correlation, and reverse causation is very much live: a video that gets pushed to a broad audience is watched by colder viewers and therefore retains worse, which would produce exactly this negative sign even if retention did help. So this does NOT prove retention is irrelevant to distribution. What it does refute is the operational form of the claim — that within a channel the higher-retention videos are the ones that get the views. In our data they are reliably the ones that get FEWER. Also: first-28d views is a demand measure, not an impressions measure; without CTR/impressions we cannot separate demand from delivery.

Look for where 50% of the viewers leave the video and go watch that part of your content to try to understand why people left at that point. You literally just lost half of your viewers.

Refuted storytelling · checked across 4 channels
Exposition should be delivered through action and choice, never through stated backstory: front-loading "why I'm doing this" in the first minute loses the audience before the story starts, and retention flattens only once the first conflict begins.
Source: Mario Joos (retention courses)
What would settle it

Has the audience already COMMITTED to finishing? Joos names the discriminator himself in 4ad699f1dd60: the YouTube intro's job is context and the rules of the world, not character exposition, 'because viewers arrive without any commitment to finish, unlike film audiences.' A cinema audience has paid and cannot leave, so early backstory is an investment; a YouTube viewer is auditing and early backstory is an unpaid bill. The second variable is what the backstory is FOR — Pixar needed the viewer to LIKE a character they would spend 100 minutes with; a YouTube creator's 'why I'm doing this' is usually a justification the viewer has not yet asked for.
