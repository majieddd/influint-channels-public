Matt Cremona, from the outside

A 447,000-subscriber woodworking channel read without any access to its analytics. The obvious reading of the catalogue is that it has been sliding since 2020, and that reading is wrong: it is measuring how long a video has had to collect views, not how well it did. What the record does show is a channel that ended a 251-video weekly treadmill in 2019, roughly tripled its running time, and now publishes mostly one-off films that are working, while the two repeatable shapes with the best evidence behind them sit unused.

The catalogue

820 produced uploads, and 125 broadcasts that had to leave first

The channel's uploads playlist holds 945 long-form videos. 125 of them carry liveStreamingDetails: Workshop Happy Hour, Ask Matt Live, shop broadcasts. Their median is 10,928 views against 52,759 for produced videos, and they run 48 minutes against 15. They are excluded here, and the exclusion is not tidiness: an outlier score divides a video by the median of its eight nearest uploads by date, so a produced film published in a run of streams was being scored against stream-sized numbers. With them in, the top of this catalogue read 281x, 251x and 216x. Those were not breakouts; that was the baseline collapsing. YouTube marks premieres the same way it marks streams, so a produced video that premiered is excluded here too.

What remains is 820 produced uploads from 2014 to 2026, a median 15 minutes long, published every 5 days. That is one of the deepest catalogues in this knowledge base.

The trend

The decline is mostly the clock, not the channel

Median lifetime views per upload peaked in 2020 at 102,427 and reads 31,591 for 2026. Taken at face value that is a channel in trouble. It is not a fair comparison: a 2020 upload has had six years to collect views and a 2026 upload has had months. Dividing each video's views by its age reverses the ranking completely, from 17,766 a year for 2020 uploads to 100,329 for 2026.

Neither number is the answer. Lifetime views flatter old videos; annualising flatters new ones, because a video's views arrive front-loaded and dividing three weeks of launch traffic by a year overstates it. The honest reading is narrower: the fall in lifetime views is substantially accrual and should not be read as decline, and nothing in public data can settle where the truth sits between the two. A first-28-day window would settle it in one query, and that needs an analytics grant.

The treadmill

251 weekly updates, and what they did to every other number

From 2014 to 2019 the channel shipped Matt's Weekly Shop Update 251 times: 31% of the produced catalogue, a median 8 minutes long, collecting 1,711 views a year against 18,283 for everything else. That is a ninefold gap, and it is the single largest fact about this catalogue.

It also quietly ruins any measurement taken over the whole catalogue. Sort by whether a title mentions slabbing, logging or sawing and those videos look 3.60x better than the rest. Remove the weekly updates, which are short, low-view and mention none of those things, and the same comparison collapses to 1.14x. The topic was never the signal; the treadmill sitting in the other group was. It survives only among the longer videos, where slabbing and logging still run 1.97x the rest over 133 uploads.

The treadmill ended in 2019. This is history, not a live problem, and no advice here rests on stopping something that stopped seven years ago.

Length

The one signal that survives every control

With the weekly updates removed, so that length is not standing in for them, views per year of life rise monotonically with runtime across 569 uploads: from 6,811 under ten minutes to 29,199 past forty-five. A 4.3x span, in order, with no bucket out of place.

He is already acting on this. The last forty uploads run a median 40 minutes against 9 in 2016. The finding is not a change to make; it is a confirmation that the change already made was the right one, and a reason not to reverse it. What this cannot tell you is whether the long videos are being watched to the end. Length correlating with views is a claim about demand, not about retention, and retention is exactly what public data cannot see.

Formats

Nine repeatable shapes, and only 6 whose numbers mean anything

The classifier found nine shapes across 462 of the 820 uploads, leaving 358 one-offs. Three of them are era-bound and their multipliers are withheld rather than printed: a shape shipped in a solid block is compared against itself, because the videos either side of it in the catalogue are the same shape. The Home Renovation series has 53% same-shape neighbours, the Gambrel barn series 74%, the weekly update 53%. For those three the honest answer is that we cannot tell, and what would make it answerable is interleaving two shapes over the same months.

The 6 readable shapes cover 94 uploads, and two of them are strong: Building a ___ at 10.41x over 13 videos with only 13% same-shape neighbours, and Ask Matt #___ at 6.06x over 22 with 9%.

The gap

The evergreen asset class he stopped making in 2018

Ask Matt #___ is twenty-two short answers to viewer questions, a median twelve minutes long, published 2014 to 2018. It is the second-best-evidenced shape on the channel at 6.06x, and eight years after the last one they are still collecting a median 11,935 views a year each. Two of them, What Does a Jointer Do? and Sharpening a Dull Chisel, are the fourth and sixth most-viewed videos on the channel at 2.36M and 1.84M.

That is what an evergreen asset looks like: a search-answering video that keeps earning without being promoted. The multiples those two carry, 157x and 129x, are the accrual effect at its most extreme and should not be read as launch performance. The durable point is the 11,935 a year, which is a rate rather than a total, and which they are still earning today.

Sawing ___ is the counter-example and the encouraging one: 22 videos from 2019 to 2026, still in use, at 2.17x and 40,588 views a year, the joint-highest rate of any shape here. The channel has a live format that works and uses it four times in its last forty uploads.

Now

A one-off channel, and it is working

Since September 2023 the channel has published 120 videos, 75 of them (62%) belonging to no repeatable shape at all. The median is 40 minutes and 32,625 views a year, the highest rate of any period in the catalogue.

The titles have changed with them. Where the catalogue's middle years are full of Matt's Weekly Shop Update - May 23, 2019 and Registration & Wrap Up - Urban Logging Trailer Build Pt 11, the recent ones read They said i needed an engineer to make this...lol, No Normal Sawyer Would Attempt This, This is Treedonculous. That is a move from cataloguing to curiosity, and the arithmetic follows it: titles carrying a number run 0.25x the rest and titles with a series suffix 0.26x, though both of those are largely the treadmill and the numbered series showing up again rather than a fact about numbers in titles.

The single best-launching video in the catalogue is the most recent one measured here: They said i needed an engineer to make this...lol, 110,210 views in about three weeks. On a per-year basis that is off the top of the chart, and on any basis it is a strong start.

Limits

What none of this can see

Every figure above is lifetime views, because that is all YouTube shows the public. There is no click-through rate, so nothing here can separate a video nobody clicked from one many clicked and left. There is no retention curve, so a forty-minute video that holds its audience and one that empties at four minutes are the same number. There is no first-28-day window, which is the measure that would settle the accrual question in the second section outright.

All three arrive together with one thing: an Analyst invitation to analytics@influint.co in YouTube Studio, or a single browser-side pull from an authenticated Studio session. Until then this is a study of demand, not of watchability, and the strongest recommendation below is deliberately the one that needs the least of what is missing.
